# Phases 2–3 — Design Execution

Every element must be driven by the brief, not by what is currently fashionable.
Trends date a mark faster than anything else; a logo is a 10-year asset.

---

## 1. Shape psychology

Shape is read before colour and long before detail. Choose the family that
reinforces the emotion fixed in Phase 1.

| Family | Reads as | Use when the emotion is |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Circle / ellipse | Friendly, whole, communal, unending | Playfulness, Care, Trust |
| Square / rectangle | Reliable, ordered, solid, institutional | Trust, Craft |
| Triangle | Dynamic, directional, tension, ascent | Innovation, Speed |
| Diagonal / slash | Motion, disruption, urgency | Speed, Innovation |
| Arc / crescent | Protective, enclosing, generous | Care |
| Hexagon / lattice | Systematic, technical, modular | Innovation, Trust |

Rules that hold regardless of family:

- **One organising geometry.** Pick a construction logic — a circle grid, a 45°
  system, a golden-ratio rectangle — and hold every curve and angle to it. Marks
  feel "designed" rather than "drawn" because of internal consistency, not
  because of complexity.
- **Counter-space is a shape too.** The gaps carry as much identity as the
  strokes. Negative-space devices are strong when they are discovered on the
  second look and invisible on the first; they are weak when they are the only
  idea.
- **Asymmetry needs a reason.** Symmetry is stable and slightly inert; asymmetry
  is energetic and slightly unstable. Choose deliberately and say why.

## 2. Colour palette

Colour is applied last and must be removable. If the mark only works in colour,
the mark does not work.

- Align hue to the emotion: deep and desaturated for exclusivity; saturated and
  bright for approachability and innovation; warm neutrals for craft.
- **Constrain the palette.** One primary, one secondary, one neutral. A third
  hue must earn its place against the blur test — at small size, three hues turn
  to mud.
- Specify colour in every space the deliverables need: HEX, RGB, and CMYK for
  print. Note that saturated RGB blues and greens will shift in CMYK; check the
  primary before committing.
- **Mandatory test:** render the mark in pure grayscale, then in pure black on
  white and pure white on black. If forms merge, tonal separation is doing work
  that shape should be doing. Fix the shape, not the colour.
- Contrast: the mark must hold at least 3:1 against every approved background,
  including the dark-mode surface.

## 3. Typography

- Choose a typeface that matches the brand's voice — formal, friendly, bold,
  refined — and the audience's expectations.

| Classification | Signals | Common fit |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Geometric sans | Rational, modern, neutral | Tech, fintech, SaaS |
| Grotesque sans | Plain, confident, industrial | Infrastructure, tooling, retail |
| Humanist sans | Warm, legible, approachable | Health, education, service |
| Transitional serif | Established, credible, editorial | Finance, law, publishing |
| Old-style serif | Heritage, provenance, craft | Food, spirits, luxury goods |
| Didone serif | Fashion, high contrast, luxury | Beauty, couture, hospitality |
| Slab serif | Sturdy, mechanical, direct | Sport, logistics, trades |

- **Never use a typeface as-is and call it a wordmark.** At minimum, correct the
  kerning by eye, adjust one or two letterforms to carry a proprietary detail,
  and set the tracking for the size it will be used at. A logotype is drawn, not
  typed.
- **Kern optically, not metrically.** Spacing is about equal *area* between
  letters, not equal distance. The classic problem pairs — `AV`, `To`, `LT`,
  `rn`, `Wa` — need manual attention.
- **One distinctive letterform is enough.** A single modified letter (a cut
  terminal, a raised crossbar, a shared stem, a ligature) gives ownership.
  Modifying every letter produces a novelty face.
- Check the wordmark at the smallest size it will ever be used, then again in a
  single weight below and above — logotypes are often deployed at weights they
  were never tested in.
- Licensing: confirm the font's licence permits logo use and outlining. Many
  free fonts do not. Final deliverables contain paths, not text, so the licence
  question is about the derivation, not the file.

## 4. Making it read as expensive

"Expensive" is a set of specific, learnable moves, not an adjective to be
prompted at a generator.

- **Restraint in count, precision in execution.** Few elements, but every
  relationship measured. Cheap looks busy; expensive looks inevitable.
- **Generous, deliberate spacing.** Wide letter tracking on a wordmark; clear
  space around the lock-up that is larger than feels necessary. Crowding reads
  as anxiety.
- **A refined palette.** Black, off-white, and one restrained accent — a muted
  metal, a deep jewel tone. Avoid pure `#000000` for large fields; a near-black
  such as `#111114` reads richer in print and on screen.
- **Optical correction over mathematical correctness.** Circles slightly
  overshoot the cap height; horizontal strokes are drawn thinner than vertical
  ones; the visual centre sits above the geometric centre. Mathematically
  perfect logos look subtly wrong.
- **Consistent stroke logic.** One stroke weight, or a deliberate two-weight
  system with a clear ratio. Random weights read as amateur.
- **No effects.** Bevels, drop shadows, inner glows, and 3D extrusion are the
  fastest way to look cheap. A gradient is permitted only if the mark is fully
  functional without it.

## 5. The anti-cliché list

Category research (Phase 2) exists to produce an avoid list. These tropes are
saturated across the board and should be rejected by default — if one is used, a
written justification is required.

- The swoosh, the orbit ring, and the abstract "connection" node graph
- The gradient blob / fluid mesh, especially purple-to-blue
- The isometric or "impossible" cube
- The generic leaf for anything claiming sustainability
- The hexagon for anything claiming to be technical
- The lightbulb for ideas, the rocket for launch, the handshake for partnership
- The letter inside a rounded square, unadorned
- Symmetrical mandala geometry with no meaning behind the symmetry
- Fake-3D ribbon knots and Möbius loops
- Centred thin-weight uppercase with wide tracking as the *entire* idea

Beyond specific shapes, watch for the structural tells of generated work: perfect
bilateral symmetry with no focal point, ornament that carries no meaning,
detail that disappears below 64px, and a mark that describes the industry
generically rather than this brand specifically.

**The test that catches it:** could this mark be swapped onto a competitor's
building with no one noticing? If yes, it is a category illustration, not a
brand.

## 6. The three routes

Present exactly three directions, each expressing a *different strategic idea*:

- **Route A — Representational.** A recognisable object or gesture from the
  brand's world, reduced to its simplest legible form.
- **Route B — Abstract/geometric.** A constructed form carrying the brand's
  quality through shape logic alone.
- **Route C — Typographic.** Monogram, letterform, ligature, or negative-space
  device built from the name itself.

For each route give: a one-line rationale tied to the brief, the shape family,
the type classification, the colour intent, and a rough 96×96 SVG so the choice
is made by eye. Three variations on the same idea is one route, not three.
